Spatial Autocorrelation of Hyperscale Data Centers and Their Community Impacts in the United States¶

Author: Shanmukh Upadhyayula

Date: 15 May 2026

Introduction¶

The rapid expansion of artificial intelligence is driving the construction of many hyperscale data centers across the United States, with their main purpose being for training AI models. These data centers are not highly clustered across the entire country, but are situated across many small hot spots. With the increasing rate of construction of hyperscale data centers, there is also an increase in protests against such data centers being created, as many people believe that the host counties of data centers face increasing housing costs, increasing electricity bills, droughts caused by data center water usage, poor air quality, and increasing electricity demand.

The motivation for this project is to attempt to answer whether these host counties are even facing such problems, if those are spatially clustered, and if there are positive or negative associations with each of these problems and hyperscale data center density. The justification for wanting to find spatial clustering and associations is that data center density is a spatial problem. Looking at raw counts or non-spatial data excludes a dimension that won't satisfy questions of whether there is clustering or associations.

The hypothesis below details the expectation, but most of these problems are not expected to be strongly tied to data center density, with the exception of electricity demand growth because that is a requirement for data centers, rather than what is seen as a side-effect. Spatial dependence and spatial heterogeneity will both be trends of interest. Spatial dependence is a trend of interest because knowing whether data center host counties share similar community impacts to their neighbors is a core part of answering the research question below. Spatial heterogeneity is a trend of interest because the distribution of data centers across the United States is very sparse, as revealed in the pre-cursor analysis to this project. Any associations with data centers and community impacts can vary greatly from region to region. For example, hot spots in Texas counties can be completely different from hot spots in Virginia. Bias is also a concern for this project. The spatial autocorrelation and spatial regression tests may reveal something completely different than the actual community impacts. This is due in part to the limitations of open source datasets available online and the data generating process. Additionally, county-level aggregation introduces the modifiable areal unit problem. One example of this problem in this project arises in the electricity demand choropleth & LISA maps. Since only state-wide data is publicly available as of May 2026, the granularity is not small enough to see truly accurate results. This is also the case for a county-level analysis as well, but aggregating to a smaller level would make map displays impractical, especially since there are approximately 85,000 census tracts in the United States. Therefore, the results should be seen as descriptive of spatial patterns rather than data center causal impacts on communities.

Research Question: Does hyperscale data center density exhibit statistically significant spatial clustering and spatial association with home value appreciation, electricity price growth, drought stress, air quality degradation, and electricity demand growth?

Hypothesis: Hyperscale data center density has almost no associations and little clustering with residential housing price appreciation, electricity price growth, drought stress, and air quality degradation at the U.S. county scale, but has a strong positive association and clustering with electricity demand growth.

Methods¶

There are five parts to this project's data analysis. First, data centers are filtered to a new gdf for just hyperscale data centers. This is because it is difficult to find an exact dataset for AI data centers online, but hyperscale data centers encompass AI data centers. These hyperscale data centers are managed by large companies. Next, the raw datasets are aggregated to US county polygons, except for electricity demand because the Energy Information Administration only reports this variable at a balancing authority level. These balancing authorities have grid topologies that don't align with county boundaries. Once the datasets are aggregated, descriptive statistics and choropleth maps are used to characterize the geographic distribution of hyperscale data center density and each community impact variable. Then, bivariate spatial autocorrelation Moran's I and LISA tests are performed to look for spatial dependence. A multivariate test with all variables is also performed. Finally, spatial regression models are applied to assess the degree to which data centers statistically associate with each community impact variable to look for spatial heterogeneity.

Data¶

The analysis uses twelve datasets: county & state boundaries (USCB 2024, 2025), global data center locations (ATLAS 2025), home price appreciation (Zillow 2026), electricity rates (Energy Information Administration 2026), county population (USCB 2023), air quality index (Environmental Protection Agency 2025), drought severity (National Drought Mitigation Center, U.S. Department of Agriculture, and National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration 2026), and electricity demand balance & subregion (Energy Information Administration 2022, 2026). Details on dataset retrieval locations can be seen in the references section.

Hyperscale data center locations are determined by if their company column is one of the major AI companies: Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta, Oracle, Apple, IBM, SoftLayer, Applied Digital, Anthropic, Fluidstack, xAI, CoreWeave, Crusoe, and Nebius. OpenAI is probably the most major AI company, but does not manufacture data centers of its own.

Most of the date ranges are from 2022 to 2026, as 2022 was the year where AI Data Centers became the most prevalent. This gives a big enough change from a time before the popularity of AI and the present day.

cd_feats = []
for st in STATE_ABBREVS:
    url = f"https://storage.googleapis.com/electricity_rates/cd_shapes/{st}.geojson"
    r = requests.get(url, timeout=30)
    if not r.ok:
        continue
    for feat in r.json()["features"]:
        props = feat["properties"]
        if not props.get("eiaids_json"):
            continue
        utilities = json.loads(props["eiaids_json"]) or []
        best = None
        for u in utilities:
            prices = u.get("prices") or []
            f22 = next((p["total"] for p in prices if p["year"] == 2022 and p["month"] == 2), None)
            f26 = next((p["total"] for p in prices if p["year"] == 2026 and p["month"] == 2), None)
            if not (f22 and f26 and f22 > 0):
                continue
            _w  = float(u.get("weight", 1) or 1)
            pct = 100 * ((f26 - f22) / f22)
            if best is None or _w > best[0]:
                best = (_w, pct, u.get("eiaid"), u.get("utility_name"))
        if best is None:
            continue
        feat["properties"]["price_pct_change"] = best[1]
        feat["properties"]["dominant_eiaid"]   = best[2]
        feat["properties"]["dominant_utility"]  = best[3]
        cd_feats.append(feat)

cd_prices  = gpd.GeoDataFrame.from_features(cd_feats, crs="EPSG:4326")
util_areas = cd_prices.dissolve(
    by="dominant_eiaid",
    aggfunc={"price_pct_change": "first", "dominant_utility": "first"},
).reset_index()

cty_ctr = counties[["GEOID", "geometry"]].copy()
cty_ctr["geometry"] = cty_ctr.geometry.centroid
cty_ctr = cty_ctr.set_crs("EPSG:4326")
joined_prices = gpd.sjoin(
    cty_ctr,
    util_areas[["dominant_eiaid", "dominant_utility", "price_pct_change", "geometry"]],

).drop_duplicates("GEOID")[
    ["GEOID", "dominant_eiaid", "dominant_utility", "price_pct_change"]
]
counties = counties.merge(joined_prices, on="GEOID", how="left")

Spatial Autocorrelation & Spatial Regression (Results)¶

Spatial weights matrix (K-Nearest-Neighbors, k=8)¶

The pre-cursor data analysis of general data centers used Queen contiguity weights and considered but rejected KNN. The reasoning there was that US county sizes vary vastly across the country, so KNN would force large rural western counties to have the same number of neighbors as small dense eastern counties, which felt like a poor match for the geography. Queen contiguity was preferred because it lets the data's natural polygon structure define the neighborhood.

That reasoning does not hold for this analysis. The variable of interest here is hyperscale data center density, which is concentrated in a small number of geographically dispersed major metros rather than spread continuously across the country. Queen contiguity is built around physical adjacency, so it can never connect two metro clusters that do not share a border, and large rural counties that separate them would simply absorb that disconnect. Queen contiguity also has a coastal boundary problem that became apparent in the pre-cursor analysis, where Miami-Dade County was not a LISA Hot spot, despite it having 47 data centers because coastal counties have fewer polygon neighbors than inland counties, giving them less statistical power in the weights calculation.

KNN with having 8 neighbors fixes both issues. Every county receives exactly 8 neighbors regardless of whether it sits on a coast or in the interior, removing the asymmetry that suppressed Miami-Dade in the pre-cursor analysis. It also allows major metros like Northern Virginia, the San Francisco Bay Area, and the Dallas-Fort Worth area to register as neighbors of one another through the nearest-neighbor graph, which better captures the spatial structure of a variable defined by a small number of dispersed clusters.

Bivariate Global Moran's I. DC density paired with each impact metric¶

Unlike univariate Moran's I, which would measure whether hyperscale data center density alone clusters in space (this test was done in the pre-cursor analysis for general data centers), the bivariate Moran's I tests done here measure the spatial clustering between hyperscale data center density and each community impact variable respectively. All five bivariate I values computed here fall very close to zero, ranging from -0.010 for drought to 0.046 for electricity demand growth. This indicates that at the national county scale, hyperscale data center density has practically no spatial co-clustering with any of the five community impact variables examined. This is partially consistent with the hypothesis. Drought and PM2.5 air quality days are not statistically significant (p = 0.118 and p = 0.215 respectively), supporting the expected absence of association for those variables. It is also expected due to those variables not having the full county counts. Home value appreciation (p = 0.022) and electricity price growth (p = 0.002) are statistically significant despite their near-zero I values, which reflects the large sample size of 3,220 counties. Electricity demand growth also has no clustering at I = 0.046 and is also significant (p = 0.001), which actually goes against the hypothesis of clustering. This is likely due to the state level aggregation, as well as the extent covering the entire country. The multivariate Moran's I among all the variables is also very low, which is expected due to the extent of the United States. It is still statistically significant (p =0.002).

Spatial regression. Effect estimation with state fixed effects¶

The Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Regression model uses spreg.OLS_Regimes with state FIPS as the regime variable and constant_regi="many", which gives each state its own intercept. This means that the model compares counties within the same state rather than across the entire country, controlling for anything that may differ between states, such as tax policy, energy regulation, and climate, that could make some states more likely to have both data centers and high impact values. The two predictors held constant across states are hyperscale data center count and log county population.

The OLS results show statistically significant hyperscale coefficients for three of the five outcomes: home value appreciation (coefficient -0.663, p = 0.020), residential electricity price growth (coefficient 0.664, p = 0.013), and drought stress (coefficient 1.223, p = 0.003). The directions are not all consistent with the community harm narrative. The negative home value coefficient suggests that within states, counties with more hyperscale data centers appreciated slightly less rather than more after controlling for population. PM2.5 air quality days and electricity demand growth are not significant. The electricity demand row shows a perfect R² = 1.000 and coefficients of essentially zero, which is the MAUP artifact described in the discussion section, since these are state fixed effects, the state aggregation makes all predictions a perfect fit. This is why spatial lag is necessary to produce more accurate predictions.

Spatial lag and spatial error models¶

The spatial lag model (spreg.GM_Lag) accounts for the fact that neighboring counties often have similar outcomes. After adding spatial lag, none of the hyperscale data center coefficients remain statistically significant, with the previously significant OLS relationships weakening to p = 0.220 for home value, p = 0.458 for electricity price, and p = 0.149 for drought.

At the same time, the spatial lag coefficients are large and highly significant for electricity price (ρ = 1.036, p = 0.000), drought (ρ = 1.051, p = 0.000), and electricity demand (ρ = 1.000, p = 0.000). This indicates that neighboring counties explain much of the variation in these outcomes, suggesting that some significant OLS results were driven more by regional spatial clustering than by hyperscale data centers themselves. This suggests that hyperscale data centers are not the main reason for certain community impacts once spatial patterns are taken into account.

Discussion and Conclusion¶

Both hyperscale data center density and the five impact variables show visible geographic clustering in the choropleth maps, with density concentrated in Northern Virginia, Texas, California, Chicago metro areas. Community impact variables are elevated across urbanized areas. This visual overlap is misleading because data centers occupy a small area of the urban counties, and the two patterns do not track each other county by county. To truly understand correlations, examining the aforementioned counties at a census tract level would be the next level of analysis.

All five bivariate global Moran's I values are extremely close to zero, indicating no meaningful spatial co-clustering nationwide. The statistically significant p-values for home value appreciation, electricity price, and electricity demand reflect the scale of the United States' 3,220 counties.

While global clustering is non-existent, LISA maps reveal localized HH co-clustering in Northern Virginia, where data center density, electricity price growth, and electricity demand growth all concentrate simultaneously in the same counties. This local signal is the strongest evidence in the analysis that data center presence is associated with community impact, even as the global measure shows no nationwide pattern.

From analyzing spatial dependence to spatial heterogeneity, OLS regression finds significant coefficients for home value appreciation, electricity price growth, and drought stress within states, but once the spatial lag model accounts for neighboring county spillovers, none of the hyperscale coefficients remain significant. This confirms that regional spatial trends in neighboring counties, not data center presence itself, drive most of the outcome variation.

Hypothesis Answer: Partially Supported. The first part of the hypothesis holds true. Home value appreciation, drought stress, and PM2.5 air quality days all show near-zero bivariate global Moran's I values and no nationwide co-clustering with hyperscale data center density, consistent with the expectation of little association. Electricity price growth also has a near-zero global Moran's I but does show localized HH co-clustering in the Northern Virginia metro, which is a partial exception. However, the second part of the hypothesis is not supported. Electricity demand growth was expected to show a strong positive association and clustering, but the global bivariate I of 0.046 is only weakly positive, but even that value partly reflects the state-level aggregation of the underlying balancing authority data rather than a true county-scale clustering signal. The only real clustering for demand appears locally in Northern Virginia, which is where hyperscale density is highest, but this falls short of the strong nationwide pattern the hypothesis predicted. If the hypothesis were to be refined, it should include distinctions between global and local clustering. In this case, HH counties like Loudoun show local co-clustering between data center density, electricity price growth, and electricity demand growth respectively. In this case, the local LISA result is the more informative measure since the relevant clustering exists at a sub-national scale.

This Northern Virginia local co-clustering pattern is consistent with spatial heterogeneity rather than nationwide spatial dependence. This is because the near-zero global Moran's I values combined with a significant local HH cluster in Loudoun and Fairfax indicate the relationship exists in specific places rather than uniformly. The high significant spillover coefficients in the spatial lag model are a separate finding showing the outcome variables themselves are spatially autocorrelated across their neighbors, not driven by hyperscale data center density.

Policy Implications. The finding that community impacts are geographically concentrated rather than nationally distributed has direct relevance for how AI infrastructure development is governed. Co-clustering of hyperscale data center density with above-average electricity prices and demand is spatially confined to a small number of metropolitan counties. The burden of rapid AI infrastructure expansion therefore falls disproportionately on those communities rather than being diffused broadly across the country. Policy on a national scale may be insufficient to address these localized harms because residents of high-density data center counties face rising utility costs and constrained grid capacity that are largely invisible in aggregate statistics. This is likely why protests against AI data centers are more prevalent in local communities rather than across the entire United States. However, the lack of nationwide clustering should not be interpreted as a lack of harmful indicators but rather as evidence that harm is local to communities. These findings would be most beneficial to local communities' policy making decisions. However, from the pre-cursor analysis, data center related jobs were measured. In that, there seemed to be no local or global clustering, but the jobs did see a rapid increase in urban areas. This indicates that data centers do have some form of positive impact on a national level, while harms are seen in local areas. These are all important factors to consider when investing in where to build an AI data center.

Data Limitation: The electricity demand growth variable is measured at the balancing authority level and assigned uniformly to all counties within a state. Spatial autocorrelation results for this variable partly reflect state boundaries rather than true county-scale co-clustering, and the p-value of 0.001 would likely not be statistically significant if measured at true county scale. Additionally, the core data center dataset is not fully up-to-date and lacks an explicit "AI" column, so hyperscale data centers are used as the closest available.

Next Steps: Next steps would include linking sub-county data at the census tract level, where impacts on housing affordability and utility costs are more likely to be detectable given how localized these effects are. In an early version of the pre-cursor analysis, a census tract level analysis was done on Loudoun county, where the univariate global Moran's I value for data centers was approximately 0.25. While this is higher than any of the bivariate global Moran's I values, it is still weak. However, even within Loudoun county, most census tracts do not have a data center. Counterintuitively, Moran's I does not automatically rise as the spatial scale becomes more granular, and this is a part of the MAUP. This project identifies local problems, at a local scale, but fails to find meaningful correlations at a national scale. The next step is to analyze the local community problems, across many communities, and try to understand the underlying causes.
